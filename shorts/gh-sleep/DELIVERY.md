## Research and fact check

| Claim in the clip | Evidence |
|---|---|
| The brain's circadian clock (the SCN, in the hypothalamus, just above the optic chiasm) sets the timing of sleep and waking. | Standard physiology: the SCN is the master clock. It shares control of sleep timing with sleep pressure (the "two-process" model). |
| When we fall asleep, brain waves slow down until we reach deep (slow-wave) sleep. | The EEG moves from fast, small waves to slow, large delta waves in N3. |
| The hypothalamus signals the pituitary; GH is released into the blood in a large pulse. | The sleep-onset pulse comes from a surge of hypothalamic GHRH while somatostatin's inhibition eases; GH comes from the anterior pituitary. ([Van Cauter & Plat 1996](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-3476%2896%2970008-2)) |
| The big pulse comes soon after falling asleep and is tied to deep sleep. | In normal sleep, a pulse begins within about 30 minutes of sleep onset, and about 70% of pulses during sleep coincide with slow-wave sleep. ([Van Cauter et al. 1992, JCI](https://www.jci.org/articles/view/119587)) |
| Delaying sleep delays the pulse with it. | Delaying sleep by about 3 hours delays the GH bursts accordingly ([Born et al. 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3406323/)). The review concludes that GH is mainly controlled by the sleep-wake cycle, not by clock time ([Leproult & Van Cauter](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2869538)). |

Limits of the wording:
- The narration says the "ระลอกใหญ่" (big pulse) is tied to sleep. It does not say GH is released only at night: smaller pulses do occur during the day.
- The graph is labelled "กราฟจำลองจากงานวิจัย" (an illustrative graph based on research). It is not real data.
